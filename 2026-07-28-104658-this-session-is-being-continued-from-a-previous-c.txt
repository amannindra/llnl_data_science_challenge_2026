  What your friend actually sent

  Not code — evidence. All 176 files are documentation, spreadsheets, images,
  and agent config. There are zero .py files, no raw scan data, and no src/
  tree. Every command in his docs (python3 -m src.part2.phase2c...) points at a
  codebase that isn't in the folder. So it's a read-only review packet: "here's
  what I found, here's the proof, now go check my top candidates by eye." That
  reframed your instruction to test every script — there were none, so I wrote
  my own verifier instead and tested that.

  What his pipeline does

  The lattice is a 9×9×9 grid of printed metal struts — 3,430 joints connected
  by 18,468 struts. He knows exactly where every strut should be from the design
  file. For each one he looks at the CT scan along that line and asks: is there
  metal here?

  Two complications. First, ~0.5% of struts were deliberately deleted at design
  time, so he has to subtract those before calling anything a defect — he found
  92 of them against an expected 92.34, which is a very clean hit. Second, the
  design and the scan are in different coordinate systems, so he had to figure
  out how to rotate one onto the other.

  His answer: 214 struts (1.16%) look like real manufacturing defects. He had a
  human eyeball the top 40 and 36 agreed. A later pass raised it to 228, and the
  14 newly-added ones are the job he's handing to you.

  The problem I'd flag first

  His own notes say the published paper found about 105 missing + 918 
  disconnected struts on this specimen — roughly 1,023, or 5.5%. He reports 214,
  or 1.16%. That's a 5× gap, mentioned exactly once, buried on line 1087 of a
  62 KB how-to file, and never revisited.

  Splitting it by type is where it gets useful:

  ┌──────────────┬───────┬──────────────┐
  │              │ paper │ his pipeline │
  ├──────────────┼───────┼──────────────┤
  │ missing      │ ~105  │ 202          │
  ├──────────────┼───────┼──────────────┤
  │ disconnected │ ~918  │ 12           │
  └──────────────┴───────┴──────────────┘

  His missing-strut detector is fine — arguably too sensitive. The entire gap is
  in "disconnected" struts, where he finds 76× fewer than the paper. The reason
  is structural: when you delete a strut from a design file you get a
  completely absent strut, so every threshold he calibrated was tuned on
  absence. There's no calibration set for partial breaks anywhere in the
  pipeline, and his gap threshold demands the void span more than half the strut
  — a real crack is short. His strict filter then cut that class by 79% (58 →
  12). That diagnosis isn't in his packet, and it's the single most actionable
  thing in the review.

  What checked out — and it's a lot

  I rebuilt his lattice from the raw JSON using this repo's own code, with no
  reference to his tables. I got 10,206 junctions → 3,430 welded nodes → 18,468 
  struts, and not just the same count — the same set of edges, element for
  element, zero on either side. His registration constants match mine to twelve
  significant figures. Two independently written codebases landing on identical
  numbers means the foundation under his defect counts is solid.

  The packet is also bit-perfect: 175 of 175 files match their SHA-256, nothing
  missing, nothing extra. The two "failures" my verifier reported are just
  MANIFEST.json containing a hash of itself, which is impossible to satisfy —
  harmless. One real gap: the 80 images behind his 40 human labels aren't
  shipped, so those labels can't be re-checked.

  The bit that surprised me

  Cross-checking his geometry found a bug in my code, not his. My tif2stl
  derives millimetres-per-lattice-unit by dividing the STL's bounding box by 18
  — but a bounding box wraps the strut surfaces while 18 units measures joint
  centres. Those differ by exactly one strut thickness. I measured it: the
  overshoot is 0.4528 mm against a known strut diameter of 0.424 mm — a match to
  half a voxel. His 2.28 is right, my 2.3052 is 1.1% too big.

  I reran with the corrected value expecting the overlap score to improve. It 
  got worse (Dice 0.4483 → 0.4263), and that turned out to be the interesting
  part: with correct scaling, more of the scan fits inside the design envelope
  but more design material has no metal behind it. That's the signature of
  struts printing thinner than designed — which is exactly the phenomenon the
  challenge is about. The old better-looking score was an undersized model
  accidentally cancelling out a real defect. I left the default alone since
  changing it revises every published metric, but the fix needs no code change:
  --mm-per-design-unit 2.28.
